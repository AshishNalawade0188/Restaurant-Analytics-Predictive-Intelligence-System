Answer:
That's a good question! It's important to clarify how SMOTE and bootstrapping typically work in this context.

SMOTE and Bootstrapping primarily address class imbalance in the target variable (rate_class in your case), not directly in individual feature columns.

When you apply SMOTE (as you did in the knn_smote_pipeline), it generates synthetic samples for the minority classes of your rate_class target. These synthetic samples will have corresponding values for all features, including online_order and book_table, which are already being one-hot encoded by your ColumnTransformer.

While the value_counts() for online_order and book_table show some imbalance within those features, the main concern for a classification model is the imbalance of the classes you are trying to predict (rate_class). SMOTE works by creating synthetic data points for the underrepresented rate_class values, and in doing so, it implicitly creates feature values (including for online_order and book_table) that are consistent with those synthetic target samples.

In summary:

Your current setup already uses OneHotEncoder for online_order and book_table, which is the standard way to prepare these categorical features for the model.
SMOTE, as applied, is already attempting to balance the rate_class by creating new synthetic samples that include values for online_order and book_table.
Directly applying SMOTE or bootstrapping to individual feature columns like online_order or book_table outside of the context of balancing the target variable is not a standard practice and might not yield desired results for your classification task.

Different parameters for SMOTE (e.g., k_neighbors).
